The difference

Same task, done two ways.

Everyone recruiting does the work on the left. Almost nobody knows the work on the right is available to them.

The person on the left is doing the motion. The person on the right is building the motion.

That shift is the whole discipline. It's called GTM engineering, but the word is misleading — the work happens in a spreadsheet, not a codebase. No CS degree. No CS classes. Every major welcome.

In practice

What you'd actually build.

You pick a space — fintech in Austin, consumer brands, VC-backed seed startups — and build the full list of companies in it. Then you find the actual person who owns hiring for the role you want, not the generic careers inbox. Then you write to them about something specific and true: a launch, a raise, a hire they just made. Most students send the same message to fifty companies. You'll send fifty different messages, and you'll build the system that makes that possible in an afternoon.

When you submit the form below, your row lands in a table that fills in the rest by itself — your major, your year, where the link that brought you here came from. We can see which channel actually sends people who show up versus which one just felt busy. Every org on campus does this part by hand in a Google Form and a group chat. It takes an afternoon to build once and it runs every semester after that.

Case competition on Monday, and you need every player in a category with headcount, funding, and what they actually sell. By hand that's a night you don't get back. As a system it's a list, a set of columns, and a re-run whenever the category changes. The same build works for a class project, a consulting case, or the market map a founder asks you for in week one of an internship.

The club

What this actually is.

Format

Working sessions, not lectures. You bring a laptop and leave with something that runs. Each session is one build, start to finish, on a real target you picked.

Cadence

Weekday evenings, weekly. The exact night isn't locked — we set it with the first cohort around their schedules. That's what the availability question below is for.

Cohort size — 15

Fifteen is the number of people one person can actually get unstuck in two hours. Bigger than that and it becomes a lecture where four people build and eleven watch. The cap is the format, not a marketing tactic.

Cost

Free. Clay supports student clubs with access to the platform, so there are no dues and nothing to buy.

There are 60+ Clay Clubs worldwide, from Warsaw to Manila. This is the UT Austin one.

Who it's for

If you're wondering whether you're technical enough — you are.

Business, Marketing, MIS, Economics, Finance, Advertising, Government, anything. The people who get the most out of this are usually the ones who are already organized and stubborn about detail — not the ones who can code.

If you can build a decent spreadsheet, you can do all of it. There's no CS prerequisite, no course requirement, and no GPA cutoff. Freshmen through grad students.

The one thing that matters: you want to leave with something built, not with a line on your résumé.
